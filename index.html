    How to use this

    1. Score the idea honestly. A 3 is not a safe default; it is a claim.
    2. Plug in unit economics, even rough guesses. Bad math is a feature: it shows what to fix.
    3. Size the market from customers up, not from analyst reports down.
    4. Run the pre-mortem, then print the one-pager and run the top experiment this week.

    Module 03

    Unit economics

    Does one customer make money, and how fast? Rough numbers are fine. The goal is to find which lever matters most before you spend on growth.

    Inputs

    $/ mo
    Average revenue per paying account. For annual plans, divide by 12.
    %
    Revenue minus cost to deliver: hosting, payment fees, support, goods, shipping.
    $
    All sales and marketing spend divided by new customers. Count your own time.
    % / mo
    Share of customers who cancel each month.
    days
    First touch to first payment. Delays revenue in the cohort chart.

    24-month cohort: 100 customers acquired in month 0

      Module 04

      Market sizing, bottom-up

      Count the customers you could actually name, multiply by what they would pay, then by the share you could realistically win.

      Inputs

      Accounts that have the problem, not people who might be interested. Source it: a registry, an association, a LinkedIn count.
      $/ yr
      %
      Share you can serve with today's product, geography, language, and channel.
      %
      Most startups win 1 to 5%. Above 10% needs a very good reason.

      Sanity checks

        Why bottom-up beats top-down

        Top-down sizing starts with an analyst number ("the wellness market is $4.4 trillion") and takes "just 1%". It sounds rigorous and tells you nothing: it hides who buys, why, and how you reach them.

        • It is falsifiable. Every input is a count or a price you can check with ten phone calls.
        • It forces a go-to-market. You cannot count customers you cannot find, so it doubles as a channel test.
        • Investors trust it. A small honest number beats a big borrowed one. "1% of a huge market" is a known red flag.
